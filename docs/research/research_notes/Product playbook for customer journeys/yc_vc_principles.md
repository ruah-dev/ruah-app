# YC and VC principles for understanding customers and designing user flows

Scope: what Y Combinator (Paul Graham, Michael Seibel, Kevin Hale, Eric Migicovsky, Gustaf Alströmer, Kat Mañalac), Sequoia (Arc), a16z, First Round Review and Techstars publish about talking to users, choosing a narrow target user, product-market fit (PMF), activation/onboarding, and rules of thumb. Everything is paraphrased; direct quotes are under 15 words. Each source is listed with its URL, author and date. The notes are aimed at Ruah's journey model (persona → goal → steps with screen, action, WHY, success signal, customer evidence).

## Q1. What do YC partners say about talking to users, doing things that don't scale, launching early, iterating, and deciding what to build?

### Takeaway
YC's position is that founders should recruit and serve the first users by hand, ask about *past concrete behaviour* rather than hypotheticals, launch early and keep launching, and let repeat usage and growth decide what to build next. Hold tightly to the problem and loosely to the solution.

### Cited Findings
**Talking to users (Eric Migicovsky, Startup School 2019)**
- He gives five core interview questions: what is the hardest part of doing X; tell me about the last time you hit this problem; why was it hard; what, if anything, have you done to solve it; what don't you love about the solutions you've tried. — [YC Week 1 Recap, 2019](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/startup-school-week-1-recap-kevin-hale-and-eric-migicovsky/)
- Mistakes to avoid: pitching your idea during the interview, asking hypotheticals instead of past specifics, and talking more than you listen. — [YC Week 1 Recap](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/startup-school-week-1-recap-kevin-hale-and-eric-migicovsky/)
- Finding early users: start with yourself and friends, go in person to where target users are, and attend industry events. — [YC Week 1 Recap](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/startup-school-week-1-recap-kevin-hale-and-eric-migicovsky/)
- He splits user conversations into three stages: (1) idea stage, find the first interested people; (2) prototype stage, pick the best first customers by cost, frequency and budget; (3) iteration stage, measure the share of users who would be "very disappointed" to lose the product, weekly, with 40%+ as a PMF signal. — [YC Week 1 Recap](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/startup-school-week-1-recap-kevin-hale-and-eric-migicovsky/)
- Techstars gives the same guidance: understand the problem before the solution, ask open questions that get people to tell a story about their world, and synthesise into Jobs-to-be-Done with functional, social and emotional dimensions. It also suggests asking what the problem going away would look like. — [Techstars Toolkit, Zach Nies](https://toolkit.techstars.com/understand-your-customers)

**Do things that don't scale (Paul Graham, 2013)**
- Founders must recruit users by hand rather than wait for them. Examples: Stripe's "Collison installation" (setting up the customer on the spot) and the Airbnb founders going door to door in New York to recruit hosts and improve listings. — [PG, Do Things That Don't Scale](https://paulgraham.com/ds.html)
- Delight early users beyond what a big company could do. Wufoo sent handwritten thank-you notes. PG says the whole experience, not only the product, should be excellent. — [PG, DTTDS](https://paulgraham.com/ds.html)
- Onboard the earliest users personally, because watching them use the product gives irreplaceable feedback. — [PG, DTTDS](https://paulgraham.com/ds.html)
- "Consulting" pattern: serve one user fully as a template for others. Viaweb built merchants' stores themselves to learn what merchants needed. — [PG, DTTDS](https://paulgraham.com/ds.html)
- Big launches matter little. Results a few months later depend more on how happy you made the first users than on how many there were. — [PG, DTTDS](https://paulgraham.com/ds.html)
- Gustaf Alströmer says founders are the ones who make startups take off. The Airbnb founders photographed listings personally and fixed reported bugs overnight. — [YC Week 4 Recap](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/startup-school-week-4-recap-kat-manalac-and-gustaf-alstromer/)

**Launching and iterating**
- Kat Mañalac (YC) wants to "destroy the idea that launching is just one moment in time." In her view launching is continuous: a silent landing page, then friends and family, then strangers (Lugg tested in IKEA parking lots), then online communities (HN, Reddit), then waitlists, social and press, pre-orders, and feature launches. — [YC Week 4 Recap](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/startup-school-week-4-recap-kat-manalac-and-gustaf-alstromer/)
- Michael Seibel's formula: choose a market where users have a real, meaningful problem, launch quickly, and listen to users. He warns that founders often "hold too tightly onto solutions and too loosely onto problems." — [Seibel, The Real PMF](https://www.michaelseibel.com/blog/the-real-product-market-fit)

**How to know what to build**
- PG treats growth as a compass: whatever produces the needed growth is right by definition, which turns the question of what to build into a weekly optimisation problem. — [PG, Startup = Growth](https://paulgraham.com/growth.html)
- PG says ideas should be noticed, not thought up. Pay attention to what annoys you. Invented "sitdown" ideas (his example is a social network for pet owners) get polite "maybe I'd use that" answers, which signal no real demand. — [PG, How to Get Startup Ideas](https://paulgraham.com/startupideas.html)
- The test question he gives: who wants this so much they will use a crappy version one? — [PG, How to Get Startup Ideas](https://paulgraham.com/startupideas.html)
- "Schlep blindness": tedious, messy problems (such as payments for Stripe) are often underserved because founders avoid them. — [PG, How to Get Startup Ideas](https://paulgraham.com/startupideas.html)
- Superhuman's roadmap rule: spend half the roadmap amplifying what the "very disappointed" users love, and half on fixing what holds back the "somewhat disappointed" users for whom the main benefit already lands. Ignore users who would not be disappointed. — [Vohra, First Round 2018](https://review.firstround.com/how-superhuman-built-an-engine-to-find-product-market-fit/)

### Inferences
- Migicovsky's five questions map directly onto Ruah's journey fields. "Last time you hit this problem" is evidence for a step. "Why was it hard" is the WHY. "What have you tried" gives the current alternative or baseline. Ruah could check that each journey's evidence is past behaviour ("did", "last time") and not a hypothetical ("would", "might").
- Checks derived from PG's DTTDS: does the journey name a *specific* first user or user class, and is there a manual or concierge step the founder can do before automating?
- Mañalac's "continuous launch" suggests each journey could have a launch or exposure status (not yet shown to users → friends → strangers → community).

### Gaps
- The full text of Seibel's "How to plan an MVP" (YC Library `6f-how-to-plan-an-mvp`) did not load, so its specific MVP rules (time-boxing, what to cut) are not captured here.
- No primary sources from Dalton Caldwell or Jared Friedman were retrieved within the tool budget.
- The talks are cited from YC's official recap posts, not full transcripts. The original talks were first given around 2016–2018 and re-recorded for Startup School 2019.

## Q2. How do YC and major VCs define product-market fit, and what are its signals?

### Takeaway
All the sources agree that PMF shows up as *pull*: repeat usage without being paid for it, retention curves that flatten, organic word of mouth, and demand the team struggles to keep up with. They differ on how to measure it (retention cohorts versus the "very disappointed" survey) and on how much market matters compared with product.

### Cited Findings
- **Andreessen (2007):** PMF means being in a good market with a product that can satisfy that market. Signs of fit: customers buy as fast as you can make the product and money piles up. Signs of no fit: customers aren't quite getting value, word of mouth isn't spreading, and usage isn't growing fast. He argues market is the most important factor. — [Andreessen, pmarchive](https://pmarchive.com/guide_to_startups_part4.html)
- **Seibel (YC):** the real signal is a frantic team struggling to keep up with growing numbers of happy, loyal, ideally paying customers. Funding and headcount are not signals. Most founders, including most YC founders, never find PMF, and claiming it too early leads to hiring, higher burn and premature optimisation. — [Seibel, The Real PMF](https://www.michaelseibel.com/blog/the-real-product-market-fit)
- **Alströmer (YC):** repeat usage is "the best most unbiased way" to tell whether people like the product. Plot cohort retention curves; good products flatten as churn stops. Registered users is a "really bad" metric. He says NPS, surveys and free-tier adoption do not correlate well with real PMF. — [YC Week 4 Recap](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/startup-school-week-4-recap-kat-manalac-and-gustaf-alstromer/)
- One Alströmer example keeps about 30% of users at month 2 and about 21% at month 20, a flattening curve. — reported in a third-party summary of his talk, [videohighlight summary](https://videohighlight.com/v/6lY9CYIY4pQ). This is secondary and not verified against the transcript.
- **Superhuman / Sean Ellis test:** ask "How would you feel if you could no longer use [product]?" (very / somewhat / not disappointed). Sean Ellis found that companies above 40% "very disappointed" tended to get traction. Superhuman went from 22% to 58% in three quarters by segmenting to the high-expectation customer (HXC) and splitting the roadmap. — [Vohra, First Round 2018](https://review.firstround.com/how-superhuman-built-an-engine-to-find-product-market-fit/). Migicovsky (YC) also endorses the weekly 40% measure — [YC Week 1 Recap](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/startup-school-week-1-recap-kevin-hale-and-eric-migicovsky/). **Contested:** Alströmer, also at YC, says surveys don't correlate well with PMF and prefers retention — [YC Week 4 Recap](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/startup-school-week-4-recap-kat-manalac-and-gustaf-alstromer/).
- **Sequoia Arc framework** has three PMF archetypes:
  - *Hair on Fire:* an urgent, obvious problem in a crowded market. Being faster or cheaper is not enough; you need a truly differentiated customer experience.
  - *Hard Fact:* customers have accepted a painful problem as the way things are. The obstacle is inertia, so the approach must be novel enough, for a problem that matters enough, to justify switching.
  - *Future Vision:* customers are oblivious or skeptical. This is the longest, riskiest path and needs commercial "pit stops" along the way.
  - Companies move between archetypes over time.
  - — [Sequoia Arc PMF Framework](https://www.sequoiacap.com/article/pmf-framework/)
- **First Round "Levels of PMF" (2024):** four levels (Nascent, Developing, Strong, Extreme), each measured on demand, satisfaction and efficiency. Examples: L1 is 3–5 customers, $0–500K ARR and 8+ week onboarding. L2 is 5–25 customers and 10–20% regretted churn. L3 is 25–100 customers, >10% referral inbound and 110%+ NRR. L4 is 100+ customers and $25M+ ARR. The four levers to move up are Persona, Problem, Promise and Product. A yellow flag is users who wouldn't be disappointed if the product disappeared tomorrow. — [First Round Levels](https://www.firstround.com/levels)
- **a16z Power User Curve:** a histogram of users by days active per month (L30), or per week (L7). A "smile" shape, with a cluster of daily users, indicates a healthy power-user core. It shows differences between users that the DAU/MAU ratio hides, and can be segmented by cohort or by specific core action. — [Jin & Chen, a16z 2018](https://a16z.com/the-power-user-curve-the-best-way-to-understand-your-most-engaged-users/)
- **a16z on AI products (2025):** early (M0–M3) retention for AI products is noisy because of "AI tourists" (hobbyists trying things cheaply). The authors suggest M12/M3 revenue retention, meaning how customers who survive tourist churn perform over the first year, as a better early predictor. — [Rodriguez & Immerman, a16z 2025](https://a16z.com/ai-retention-benchmarks/)

### Inferences
- A journey's "success signal" should ideally be a *repeat* behaviour at the product's natural frequency (see Q3), not a one-time event such as signing up. Ruah could flag success signals that are vanity events (registered, visited page) and suggest a retention-type alternative.
- Sequoia's archetype is a useful per-persona tag. Hair-on-Fire journeys need a very fast path to the differentiated experience. Hard-Fact journeys need a WHY that overcomes inertia (switching cost, a reason to act now). Future-Vision journeys need education steps.
- For a solo developer, First Round's numeric thresholds (ARR, team size, burn multiple) mostly don't apply. The qualitative signals (regretted churn, referral share, onboarding length, falling customisation requests) do.

### Gaps
- The Sequoia page is dated April 2024. The framework may have been published earlier on Arc and re-dated; the original publication date is unconfirmed.
- I found no single public "official" YC numeric retention threshold (such as "X% at month 6").
- a16z's M12/M3 benchmark values were not visible in the extracted text.

## Q3. What do these sources say about onboarding, activation, time-to-value, and the first session?

### Takeaway
Early on, the published advice is to onboard by hand (the founder does it), learn from those sessions what the "aha" and "habit" moments are, and only then encode them into an opinionated, interruptive, interactive in-product flow. Measure activation as reaching repeated core value at the product's natural frequency, not as completing sign-up.

### Cited Findings
- PG: onboard the earliest users personally, and make the whole experience excellent, not only the product. — [PG, DTTDS](https://paulgraham.com/ds.html)
- Stripe's "Collison installation" meant the founders set up the integration on the customer's laptop on the spot, removing the time-to-value gap by hand. — [PG, DTTDS](https://paulgraham.com/ds.html)
- Superhuman's activation stages (borrowed from Reforge) are: Signed up → Setup Moment → Aha Moment → Habit Moment → Engaged. — [First Round, Superhuman onboarding playbook 2025](https://review.firstround.com/superhuman-onboarding-playbook/)
- Human-led 1:1 onboarding at Superhuman: 65%+ of customers fully switched their email, roughly 2x the activation of self-serve, with about 2x the referrals. Founders and early staff onboarded hundreds of users personally before hiring specialists. — [First Round, Superhuman onboarding](https://review.firstround.com/superhuman-onboarding-playbook/)
- They learned what to teach through 30-minute discovery conversations, each producing pages of notes, 5–10 feature requests and bugs. They identified the cases where human guidance helps most: workflow changes, new interaction paradigms, requests for specific features, and habit building. — [First Round, Superhuman onboarding](https://review.firstround.com/superhuman-onboarding-playbook/)
- Moving to product-led onboarding took about three years of turning those lessons into product. Three principles:
  - *Opinionated:* there is likely a best path; strip the product down to essentials.
  - *Interruptive:* full-screen steps instead of checklists raised completion from 30% to 98%.
  - *Interactive:* users practise in a synthetic sandbox, and features are taught right after the relevant action.
  - — [First Round, Superhuman onboarding](https://review.firstround.com/superhuman-onboarding-playbook/)
- Alströmer's "natural frequency": pick the core value metric that matches how often the product is naturally used. Examples: Airbnb yearly, Instagram daily, Gusto every two weeks, Lyft weekly or monthly. — [YC Week 4 Recap](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/startup-school-week-4-recap-kat-manalac-and-gustaf-alstromer/)
- First Round lists long onboarding (8+ weeks) and heavy customisation requests as efficiency signs of only Level-1 PMF. Shorter onboarding and fewer customisation requests show up at higher levels. — [First Round Levels](https://www.firstround.com/levels)
- a16z suggests tracking deeper *core actions*, not logins, in engagement histograms, to see which actions indicate real value. — [Jin & Chen, a16z](https://a16z.com/the-power-user-curve-the-best-way-to-understand-your-most-engaged-users/)

### Inferences
- A journey template can mirror the stages: each journey marks which step is the Setup moment, the Aha moment (first delivery of the WHY) and the Habit moment (repeat at the natural frequency). Ruah could check that every primary journey has an identified Aha step and a success signal at the natural cadence.
- "Opinionated" onboarding suggests checking step count and branching before the Aha step: fewer steps and one default path.
- "Interactive/teach after action" means the step's screen and action should put guidance inside the flow at the moment it's relevant, not in upfront tours.
- For a solo dev, "do it by hand first" becomes: record the manual onboarding sessions as customer evidence, and derive the journey steps from them.

### Gaps
- YC's own published material (in what I could retrieve) says little that is specific about in-product onboarding UX or numeric time-to-value targets. Most concrete onboarding guidance here comes from First Round/Superhuman, and the activation-stage model comes from Reforge.
- No a16z or YC numeric "time-to-first-value" benchmark was found.

## Q4. What do they say about choosing a narrow target user/persona, and about "why" (severity, frequency, who has it most)?

### Takeaway
Start with a small group who want the product badly ("deep well") rather than many who want it a little. Choose problems that are urgent, frequent, expensive or mandatory. Define the persona from your most enthusiastic users (the HXC) in their own words, and narrow until the product is loved.

### Cited Findings
- PG: a good idea is wanted a lot by a small number of people, not a little by many. His image is a deep, narrow well rather than a shallow crater. — [PG, How to Get Startup Ideas](https://paulgraham.com/startupideas.html)
- PG: start with a contained market to build critical mass (Facebook at Harvard only). — [PG, DTTDS](https://paulgraham.com/ds.html)
- Kevin Hale (YC): good problems are popular, growing (about 20% a year), urgent, expensive, mandatory, and/or frequent (hit "multiple times a day"). Ideally a problem has several of these. — [YC Week 1 Recap](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/startup-school-week-1-recap-kevin-hale-and-eric-migicovsky/)
- Hale frames an idea as problem + solution + insight (unfair advantage). He lists five kinds of advantage: founder, market, product (10x better), acquisition (word of mouth), and monopoly or network effects. — [YC Week 1 Recap](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/startup-school-week-1-recap-kevin-hale-and-eric-migicovsky/)
- Seibel: look for customers whose hair is on fire, meaning desperate enough to use an imperfect v1. — [Seibel, The Real PMF](https://www.michaelseibel.com/blog/the-real-product-market-fit)
- Migicovsky: at the prototype stage, pick the best first customers by how costly and frequent the problem is and whether they have budget. — [YC Week 1 Recap](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/startup-school-week-1-recap-kevin-hale-and-eric-migicovsky/)
- Superhuman: build a high-expectation customer (HXC) profile from the "very disappointed" users, *in their language*, and narrow the market to that segment. — [Vohra, First Round 2018](https://review.firstround.com/how-superhuman-built-an-engine-to-find-product-market-fit/)
- First Round's Persona lever asks who benefits most from your insight and whether the persona is too narrow, too broad or should be dropped. The Problem lever says to solve painkiller problems, not vitamins. Ironclad found its niche (legal ops, routine contracts) by watching customers use the product. — [First Round Levels](https://www.firstround.com/levels)
- Techstars: synthesise interviews into functional, social and emotional dimensions (Jobs to be Done) of the progress the customer wants. — [Techstars Toolkit](https://toolkit.techstars.com/understand-your-customers)

### Inferences
- The "WHY" field of a journey step can be structured and checked with Hale's attributes (urgent / frequent / expensive / mandatory) plus Techstars' JTBD dimensions (functional, social, emotional). Ruah could warn when a WHY has none of them ("nice to have" or vitamin).
- A persona should be defined by behaviour and pain (who has it most, how often), ideally in quotes from evidence, not by demographics. Ruah could flag personas with no linked evidence and personas described in the builder's words rather than the customer's.
- "Deep well" suggests one primary persona per product at early stage. Ruah could nudge when a project defines many personas with no primary.

### Gaps
- None of the retrieved sources gives a numeric rule for how narrow a persona should be, or how many interviews are enough. Techstars' toolkit does not specify an interview count.

## Q5. What concrete rules of thumb or numbers do they give?

### Takeaway
The most-cited numbers are PG's 5–7% weekly growth, Sean Ellis's 40% "very disappointed" threshold, flattening retention curves, and First Round's staged PMF benchmarks. Most carry dating or context caveats.

### Cited Findings
- **Weekly growth (PG, 2012):** 5–7% a week is good, 10% exceptional, and 1% means you haven't figured it out. Measure revenue, or active users before monetisation. 1% a week is about 1.7x a year; 5% a week is about 12.6x a year. Caveats he gives: don't buy users above lifetime value or game the metric. — [PG, Startup = Growth](https://paulgraham.com/growth.html)
- **Compounding from manual recruiting:** 10% weekly growth compounds to large numbers within a year (PG's example is about 14,000 users). — [PG, DTTDS](https://paulgraham.com/ds.html)
- **40% "very disappointed"** (Sean Ellis, via Superhuman and Migicovsky): measure weekly, monthly or quarterly. Superhuman went from 22% to 58%. — [Vohra, First Round](https://review.firstround.com/how-superhuman-built-an-engine-to-find-product-market-fit/); [YC Week 1 Recap](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/startup-school-week-1-recap-kevin-hale-and-eric-migicovsky/)
- **Roadmap split:** 50% amplify what users love, 50% remove blockers. — [Vohra, First Round](https://review.firstround.com/how-superhuman-built-an-engine-to-find-product-market-fit/)
- **Market growth:** Hale's "growing market" is about 20% a year. The product advantage should be about 10x better. — [YC Week 1 Recap](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/startup-school-week-1-recap-kevin-hale-and-eric-migicovsky/)
- **Growth channels:** most successful companies dominate 1–2 channels. Don't work on growth until some people care about the product. — [YC Week 4 Recap](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/startup-school-week-4-recap-kat-manalac-and-gustaf-alstromer/)
- **First Round PMF levels:** L2 regretted churn is 10–20%. L3 has under 10% regretted churn, 110%+ NRR and over 10% referral inbound. L4 has 120%+ NRR and 13%+ referral-driven leads. Onboarding of 8+ weeks is an L1 sign. — [First Round Levels](https://www.firstround.com/levels)
- **Onboarding completion:** full-screen required steps raised completion from 30% to 98% compared with checklists (Superhuman). Human onboarding gave about 2x activation. — [First Round, Superhuman onboarding](https://review.firstround.com/superhuman-onboarding-playbook/)
- **DAU/MAU:** third-party summaries often cite 20%+ as good for social apps and 10–15% for B2B SaaS. This came from search-result aggregation, not a retrieved a16z primary page, so treat it as unverified. a16z's own point is that DAU/MAU hides differences between users and should be paired with the L30 histogram. — [a16z Power User Curve](https://a16z.com/the-power-user-curve-the-best-way-to-understand-your-most-engaged-users/)

### Inferences
- For a solo developer, the most portable rules are: a weekly growth or retention target on a single core-value metric at the natural frequency; a periodic 40% survey of the few active users; and "don't work on growth or scale until some users would be very disappointed."
- Ruah's journey "success signal" could carry a target (a threshold plus a cadence), echoing these rules of thumb, while noting that the numbers are heuristics.

### Gaps / dated or contested advice
- **Dated:** PG's 5–7% weekly growth (2012) comes from the venture-scale YC batch context and is measured over the ~3-month batch. It is not a norm for bootstrapped or indie products. Andreessen's essay is from 2007, and his claim that "market matters most" is disputed by team-first investors. The Startup School talks cited are from 2019 recaps.
- **Contested:** the survey-based 40% test (Ellis/Vohra/Migicovsky) versus Alströmer's view that surveys and NPS don't correlate well with PMF and retention should be used instead. Early retention in AI products is distorted by "tourists" (a16z 2025).
- **Context-bound:** First Round's level benchmarks are B2B/enterprise (ARR, NRR, burn multiple). Superhuman's human-led onboarding was costly and premium, around $650K ARR per specialist per year.
- **Not found:** an official YC retention percentage target, a YC-published time-to-value target, or a recommended number of interviews.
